Beyond Navigation: The Growing Value of Street View Mapping Data
JSAN Consulting
June 12, 2026
5 min read

Open a digital map today and it’s easy to take the underlying data for granted.

Addresses appear where they should. Businesses are discoverable within seconds. Routes are calculated instantly. Entire streets can be explored long before anyone physically visits them.

Behind those experiences sits an enormous amount of geospatial data, much of it collected through street view mapping and 360° mobile mapping programmes.

While these datasets are most commonly associated with navigation, organisations across telecom, utilities, transportation and government are increasingly finding value in them long after the original mapping programme has been completed.

A dataset collected once may go on to support infrastructure planning, utility mapping, telecom network assessments, road asset management, Points of Interest (POI) databases and a growing range of location intelligence initiatives.

Having delivered over one million kilometres of 360° street-view mapping across more than 20 countries, we’ve seen this shift first-hand. The conversation is no longer just about collecting imagery. It’s about how much value can be extracted from the data after collection.

More Than a Digital Window Into the Real World

The term “street view mapping” can sometimes be misleading because it suggests the primary output is imagery.

In reality, every survey creates a detailed record of the surrounding environment. Road geometry, lane information, road signs, utility poles, telecom infrastructure, street furniture, building attributes, access restrictions and thousands of Points of Interest become part of the dataset.

Viewed individually, these may seem like routine observations. Together, they create a highly detailed representation of how infrastructure and the built environment exist on the ground at a specific point in time.

This is what makes street-level data so versatile. A dataset originally captured to improve navigation can later support entirely different operational objectives without requiring additional field collection.

One Dataset, Different Perspectives

One of the most interesting aspects of street view mapping is that different organisations often look at the same dataset and see completely different things.

A telecom planner may focus on poles, cabinets and available infrastructure that could support future fibre deployment. A utility operator may be interested in asset visibility, accessibility and surrounding conditions. A navigation provider may focus on road geometry, lane-level information, address validation and POI accuracy. For municipalities, the same dataset may support road asset inventories, signage management and urban planning initiatives.

The underlying imagery remains the same, but the value changes depending on the question being asked.

This ability to support multiple use cases from a single collection programme is one of the reasons street-level mapping has become increasingly important. Rather than existing as a standalone product, it is becoming a foundational dataset that can support different departments, workflows and long-term planning activities.

Why It Matters More Today

The growing importance of street-level data reflects a broader challenge facing infrastructure organisations.

Networks are expanding. Cities are becoming more complex. Asset inventories are growing larger and more difficult to maintain. At the same time, organisations are expected to make faster decisions based on more accurate information.

Understanding what exists on the ground has become increasingly important across sectors.

Street view mapping helps provide that context by capturing infrastructure within its real-world environment. Roads, utilities, telecom assets, buildings and public infrastructure do not operate independently. They exist within connected systems, and understanding those relationships is often critical for effective planning and decision-making.

As organisations invest in digital infrastructure, location intelligence and more data-driven operations, the value of accurate street-level datasets continues to grow.

Looking Ahead

Perhaps the most significant change over the past decade is not how street-level data is collected, but how it is used.

A mapping programme commissioned for one purpose may continue creating value years later through entirely different applications. Navigation may be the starting point, but it is increasingly just one part of a much larger story.

At JSAN, we’ve seen organisations derive value from the same dataset across infrastructure planning, utility mapping, telecom projects, road asset management and location intelligence initiatives. The collection happens once, but the opportunities to use the data continue to expand.

As mapping technologies evolve and infrastructure becomes more interconnected, the future value of street view mapping is unlikely to be measured solely by coverage or imagery quality.

It will be measured by how many decisions a single dataset can help inform.
